On screen · press P — the issue

But here's what we got wrong when we started. We assumed all stress was bad. It isn't. Researchers call the useful kind eustress. It sharpens your focus, it builds confidence, and it stops when the task stops. Everybody needs some of it.

The problem is the other kind — the kind that never switches off. And from the outside, the two look identical. That's why it gets missed. Someone's marks can hold steady for months while they quietly fall apart underneath, which is why the students staff worry about last are often the ones struggling most.

Speaker 43:52 – 5:00

On screen · press P — the plan

So here's Uplift. Trained senior students run six weeks of short sessions for younger year levels. Each session does two things — one practical strategy, like planning or breaking work down, and one honest conversation.

Both halves are backed by research. Study skills training measurably cuts academic stress. And near-peers get through where adults don't — in one controlled trial, trained student leaders got friends to a trusted adult four times more often than untrained students did.

Right now those two things happen in separate rooms, taught by adults, to students who have already decided the problem is theirs alone. We put them in the same room, led by someone two years older who says out loud that they found it hard too.

Wellbeing staff train the leaders, so nobody is ever asked to be a counsellor. It launches with an assembly where seniors tell the truth about their own stress. And every group trains the next one, so it keeps running after we leave.

If someone asks a question

Isn't this just peer mentoring?
Peer mentoring is usually general wellbeing. Ours is specifically about academic pressure, and every session pairs a practical study strategy with the conversation. That combination is the part that doesn't currently exist.
What if a peer leader can't handle it?
They're never asked to. Training sets a hard line and names the staff member to hand over to, sessions are supervised, and referring someone up is treated as the program working, not failing.
How is this different from the counsellor?
It isn't a replacement — it's the step before. Only one in five students who need help actually go. We're trying to reach the other four, and get them to the counsellor when they need it.
Where does the time come from?
Six weeks, thirty to forty minutes. That fits an existing pastoral slot rather than needing a new one, and the research says short and regular is the most effective shape anyway.
What if it doesn't work?
Then we'll know, because we're measuring it with the same validated survey before and after. If the numbers don't move, we change the sessions.
Why students and not teachers?
Because that's the whole point. Only one in five students who needed help went to an adult. Trained student leaders got friends to an adult four times more often than untrained students. The near-peer part isn't decoration — it's the mechanism.
